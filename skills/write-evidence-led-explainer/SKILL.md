# Write an evidence-led explainer

Make a reader understand what was asked, how it was tested, what the evidence shows, and where the conclusion stops. Match the user's language, audience, length, and publication format.

## Shape the story

1. Open with a specific question or surprising observation and say why it matters. If the piece is long, give a brief answer near the start: question, approach, main finding, and key qualification.
2. Follow the actual inquiry: idea or hypothesis, method, observations, interpretation, and limits. Arrange sections around questions a reader would naturally ask. Do not force these headings or a full research arc onto a short piece.
3. Explain each technical idea in plain language before naming it. Use a concrete example, visual comparison, or familiar analogy when it reveals the mechanism. Add formulas or implementation detail only when the audience needs them; put optional depth in a separate section if the format allows.
4. Let the reader inspect the evidence. State the comparison, baseline, sample, measurement, and meaningful uncertainty close to the result. Use a chart, table, or interactive example only when it helps the reader see a relationship that prose alone hides. Never describe an imagined demo as working.
5. Write transitions that carry the investigation forward: what the last result established, what remains unexplained, and why the next check matters. Short sentences can mark a finding; surrounding prose should explain it.
6. End with what the result means in practice, followed by the strongest limitations or alternative explanations. Separate observation from inference and open question. Credit sources and methods at the level the format needs.

## Voice and evidence

- Sound curious, direct, and restrained. First person is useful when it clarifies what the author actually did or noticed; do not invent personal experience.
- Prefer ordinary verbs and concrete nouns to jargon or hype. Define necessary terms once, then use them consistently.
- Preserve the strength of the evidence. Do not turn a correlation into a cause, a small test into a universal rule, or an estimate into a fact. If evidence is missing, say what remains untested.
- Keep important numbers in context: compared with what, under which conditions, and with what uncertainty. Avoid a pile of figures without a takeaway.
- Preserve the user's facts and intent when revising. Verify source-dependent claims when access is available; never invent data, quotes, references, or working code.

Before delivering, check whether a reader can retell the question, test, main result, and main limitation without decoding specialist language.

## Inspiration

The presentation pattern is adapted from Vladyslav Hamolia's [“Every iPhone has a fingerprint. Can one photo give you away?”](https://vhamolya.com/blog/which-iphone-took-this-photo/): an early short version, a visible experiment, plain-language explanations, measured results, and explicit limits. Use these as writing decisions, not as text to copy or a mandatory outline.
