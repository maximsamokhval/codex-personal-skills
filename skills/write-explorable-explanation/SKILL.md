# Write an explorable explanation

Make a claim understandable, inspectable, and open to a reader's own questions. The prose carries the argument on its own; interaction lets the reader test a parameter, comparison, or example without leaving the story. Match the user's language, audience, medium, and length.

## Build the argument and the exploration together

1. Start with a concrete question or surprising observation. Give a short answer near the start of a long piece, including its main qualification.
2. Identify the few claims that a reader should be able to test. For each, define the evidence or model, the comparison that matters, and what might change the conclusion. Do not add controls just to make the page look interactive.
3. Write each exploration as part of the narrative. First tell the reader what to notice or predict; then let them change one meaningful thing; then explain what the resulting change shows. Keep the default state useful, so the article still makes sense without touching a control.
4. Specify the interaction in concrete terms when authoring its script: input or control, starting value and range, output that updates, comparison or baseline, and the insight or limit revealed. Make assumptions, units, and data sources visible. If the deliverable is prose only, mark proposed interactions as proposals; do not imply they already work.
5. Reveal the mathematics in layers. Explain the mechanism with a concrete example before naming variables. Show the formula or calculation when it supports the claim, and connect every symbol to something the reader can see or change. Keep the exact relationship and its uncertainty; plain language must not weaken the proof.
6. Let each result create the next question. Move through hypothesis, method, observation, interpretation, and limits as the inquiry requires, without forcing those headings onto every piece. End with what the evidence means and where it stops.

## Write in plain language

- Use short, direct sentences and concrete nouns. Give each paragraph one job. Remove filler, hype, and decorative jargon; define necessary terms once.
- Use specific headings and captions that say what a reader should learn, not labels such as "Chart 1" or "Interesting result".
- Put the key comparison beside the figure or interaction. State sample size, baseline, conditions, and meaningful uncertainty close to the result.
- Separate measured results from interpretation and open questions. Do not turn correlation into cause, an illustrative simulation into empirical evidence, or a small sample into a general law.
- Use first person only for work the author actually did. Preserve the user's facts and intent; never invent data, working interactions, citations, or personal experience.

## Check the reader's path

The reader should get the main answer by reading alone. By changing an input, they should be able to ask a real follow-up question and see the consequence. Check that every control advances the argument, every mathematical claim can be traced to evidence or stated assumptions, and the strongest limitation is easy to find.

## Sources of the approach

- [Bret Victor, “Explorable Explanations”](https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/): author-guided active reading, with models and assumptions the reader can inspect and adjust.
- [Vladyslav Hamolia, “Every iPhone has a fingerprint. Can one photo give you away?”](https://vhamolya.com/blog/which-iphone-took-this-photo/): a short answer followed by interactive demonstrations, measured results, technical detail on demand, and explicit limits.

Adapt the approach to the assignment. Do not copy either source's prose, visuals, or structure verbatim.
